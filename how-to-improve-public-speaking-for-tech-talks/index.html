How to improve your public speaking for tech talks: structure, nerves, filler words, and the practice that actually moves the needle

By Pranjul RathourUpdated 2026-09-248 questions answered1,359 words

Key takeaways

  • Generic public-speaking advice, power poses, breathing tricks, helps less for a technical talk than structuring the content so the room can follow it.
  • Nerves do not go away with experience; they get quieter because preparation removes the specific things you are actually afraid of, forgetting what's next or losing the room.
  • Filler words drop dramatically once you replace them with a deliberate pause, which also makes you sound more confident, not less.
  • Rehearsing out loud, not silently reading slides, is the single highest-leverage practice habit, and most people skip it.
  • Reading a room in real time, confusion, boredom, engagement, and adjusting pace or depth accordingly, is a trainable skill, not a fixed trait.

How do I actually get better at public speaking for tech talks?

By practising the specific talk out loud, repeatedly, more than by practising "public speaking" as a general skill. Generic advice, power poses, deep breathing, helps a little with nerves in the moment, but the bigger lever for a technical talk is structure: a clear problem, a logical build, a strong close, rehearsed enough times that you are not simultaneously thinking about content and delivery. Most improvement comes from removing the specific things you are afraid of, forgetting what comes next, losing the room, running over time, one rehearsal at a time.

I have given technical talks to rooms from twenty to several hundred students, and the improvement that actually showed up in feedback came almost entirely from rehearsal discipline, not from any general public-speaking technique.

Confidence on stage is not a personality trait. It is the residue of having rehearsed the specific talk enough times that nothing in it can surprise you. Pranjul Rathour

Why does structure matter more than delivery style for a technical talk?

Because an audience that cannot follow the logical thread of a talk will not be won over by good delivery, and an audience that can follow a clear structure will forgive an imperfect delivery. A technical talk needs a stated problem, a build toward the solution with one idea per section, and a close that restates what changed for the listener. Delivery, pace, tone, eye contact, matters, but it amplifies a structure that already works; it cannot rescue a talk that has no clear thread [3][4].

How do I handle nerves before and during a talk?

Nerves rarely disappear with experience; they get quieter because thorough preparation removes the specific fears driving them. Rehearse the opening thirty seconds until it is close to memorised, since a strong start calms the rest of the talk. Arrive early enough to test the room's tech, slides, mic, clicker, so equipment surprises do not add to the nerves. Accept a slightly faster heartbeat as normal rather than fighting it, and use the first minute's adrenaline for energy rather than trying to suppress it entirely.

How do I cut filler words like "um" and "so"?

Replace the filler with a deliberate silent pause instead of trying to suppress it through willpower alone; a pause reads as confidence, while "um" reads as searching. Filler words cluster at points where you have not fully decided what comes next, so the real fix is often tightening your structure and rehearsal, not a speech habit in isolation. Recording yourself and counting fillers in a five-minute segment makes the habit visible, which is usually enough to start correcting it, since most people underestimate how often they do it until they hear it back.

What is the single most effective practice habit?

Rehearsing the actual talk out loud, standing, at full volume, as many times as the stakes justify, rather than silently reading slides or rehearsing only in your head. Silent rehearsal does not train the muscle memory of speaking, the timing, or where you actually stumble; those only show up when you say the words aloud. Recording one full out-loud rehearsal and watching it back, however uncomfortable, surfaces more fixable problems in ten minutes than a week of silent preparation.

How do I read a room and adjust in real time?

Watch for the visible signals: heads down on phones, note-taking, confused frowns, nodding along, and adjust pace or depth accordingly, slowing down and adding an example when you see confusion, moving faster when you see impatience. This is trainable by deliberately watching the room instead of only your slides or notes during rehearsal-adjacent early talks, and by asking a trusted person in the audience to flag specific moments afterward where the room's energy visibly shifted.

What mistakes do people make trying to improve their public speaking?

Practising general public-speaking exercises instead of the actual talk they need to give. Reading slides silently and calling it rehearsal. Trying to eliminate nerves entirely instead of channelling them. Adding more content when a talk runs long instead of cutting it, which makes nerves worse under time pressure. Never recording themselves, so the gap between how a talk feels and how it actually sounds stays invisible.

Public speaking interview and evaluation questions

"Tell me about a time you presented to a group" is asked in almost every interview, technical or not, and the strongest answers describe a specific talk, its structure, and what you changed after getting feedback, not a general claim of being "good at public speaking". Toastmasters-style structured feedback, or simply asking a trusted listener for one specific thing to improve, builds this evidence faster than unstructured practice alone [1].

Where should I start?

Record yourself giving your next talk's opening two minutes, out loud, standing, and watch it back once. That single exercise usually surfaces more than a week of reading advice.
