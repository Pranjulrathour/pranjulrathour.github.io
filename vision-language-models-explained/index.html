Vision language models explained: vs LLMs, examples, open-source picks, Ollama, CLIP, VLA

Key takeaways

  • VLMs combine a vision encoder with a language model to answer questions about images, documents and charts.
  • VLMs are a vision-and-language subset of multimodal models, which can span audio, video and actions.
  • Strong open VLMs include Qwen-VL, InternVL, Gemma 3 and Llama 3.2 Vision; Ollama runs several locally.
  • Interpretability heatmaps suggest VLMs sometimes answer from language priors instead of image evidence.
  • CLIP trained its own text encoder for contrastive alignment; later work added LLM text encoders.

A vision language model, VLM, is an AI model that takes images and text as input and produces text, so it can describe photos, read documents, answer questions about charts and reason about what it sees. Most combine a vision encoder with a large language model. I build vision and document systems, including FaceVision and DocuLens AI, so this page explains VLMs, how they differ from LLMs and multimodal models, examples, open-source and Ollama choices, where they look, CLIP's design and courses.

What are vision language models?

A VLM pairs a vision encoder, often a Vision Transformer trained like CLIP or SigLIP, with a language model. The encoder turns an image into visual tokens, a small projector maps them into the language model's space, and the LLM reads them with your text.

Also asked as: vision language models explained

Training usually has stages: first align vision features with language using image-caption pairs, then instruction-tune on question-answer data about images, documents and charts, sometimes followed by preference tuning. The result can caption images, answer visual questions, extract fields from forms, read handwriting and explain diagrams. Limitations include hallucinating objects not present, struggling with counting and fine spatial details, and sensitivity to image resolution, so validate outputs for important uses.

Vision language model vs LLM: what is the difference?

An LLM processes only text. A VLM adds visual input, so it can answer questions about images, screenshots and documents. Many VLMs are built by attaching a vision encoder to an existing LLM, so their text abilities are similar.

The visual pathway adds costs: images consume many tokens, raising latency and cost, and the model may be slightly weaker on pure text tasks than its text-only base if training traded off capabilities. VLMs can do things LLMs cannot, such as reading a chart or checking a UI screenshot. For document work, a VLM can sometimes replace OCR plus an LLM, though validation remains important, which is how DocuLens AI treats extracted fields.

Vision language model vs multimodal model: what is the difference?

A VLM is a type of multimodal model focused on vision and language. Multimodal models more broadly can handle any combination of text, images, audio, video and sometimes actions, as input or output.

Some frontier models are natively multimodal, trained on several modalities together and able to accept audio and video, and sometimes generate images or speech. Classic VLMs accept images and output text only. Image generators such as diffusion models are also multimodal, text in and images out, but are not VLMs. When choosing a model, check exactly which inputs and outputs it supports, since marketing terms vary between providers and versions.

What are some examples of vision language models?

Proprietary examples include GPT-4o and later OpenAI models, Claude and Gemini, all of which accept images. Open examples include LLaVA, the Qwen-VL family, InternVL, Llama 3.2 Vision, Gemma 3, Pixtral, Molmo, PaliGemma, SmolVLM and MiniCPM-V.

They serve different needs: large proprietary models lead on difficult reasoning and document understanding; Qwen-VL and InternVL are strong open models for documents and charts; SmolVLM and Moondream are tiny models for edge devices; PaliGemma is designed for fine-tuning on specific tasks; and Florence-2 handles captioning, detection and OCR-style tasks with a small footprint. Specialised document models also exist for converting pages into structured text or Markdown.

What are the best open-source vision language models?

Strong open choices include the latest Qwen-VL models, InternVL, Gemma 3, Llama 3.2 Vision, Molmo and Pixtral for general use, plus SmolVLM and Moondream for small devices. Rankings change quickly, so check current leaderboards.

Also asked as: best vision language models open source

Hugging Face's Open VLM Leaderboard and benchmarks such as MMMU, DocVQA, ChartQA and OCRBench compare models, but test on your own images, since benchmark strengths differ from real tasks. Consider licence terms, which vary, model size versus your GPU memory, and support in serving tools such as vLLM, llama.cpp and Ollama. For document extraction, prioritise models strong on OCR benchmarks; for chat about photos, general reasoning benchmarks matter more.

What is the best vision language model on Ollama?

Good Ollama options include Gemma 3, Qwen2.5-VL, Llama 3.2 Vision, MiniCPM-V, LLaVA and Moondream. Pick by hardware: small models such as Moondream or 4-billion-parameter Gemma 3 run on laptops, while larger ones need more memory.

Run one with a command such as ollama run gemma3, then pass an image path in the prompt, or send images as base64 through Ollama's API from Python or JavaScript. For document reading, Qwen2.5-VL is often strong; for general chat about photos, Gemma 3 and Llama 3.2 Vision perform well. Test several on your own images, comparing accuracy and speed, and keep image resolution reasonable, since very large images slow inference.

Where do large vision language models look when answering questions?

Researchers study this with heatmaps from attention weights or gradient-based attribution, showing which image regions most influence an answer. A 2025 paper with this exact title proposed a method for visualising these regions across many models.

Also asked as: where do large vision language models look at when answering questions

Such interpretability studies find that models do not always focus on the evidence a human would use; sometimes they answer from language priors, guessing from the question, with little reliance on the image. Higher input resolution and better vision encoders tend to improve grounding on small details. For practitioners, the lesson is to test with images where the correct answer differs from common expectations, and to ask models for bounding boxes or quotes as evidence.

Why doesn't CLIP use a pretrained LLM as its text encoder?

CLIP, released by OpenAI in 2021, trained a modest transformer text encoder from scratch jointly with the image encoder, because contrastive learning needed text and image embeddings shaped together, and captions are short.

Also asked as: why doesn't clip use a pretrained large language model as the text encoder

Large decoder LLMs are optimised to predict the next token, not to produce one compact embedding for a sentence, and running a huge model for every caption in hundreds of millions of training pairs would have been very expensive. Later work explored the idea: LiT locked a pretrained image encoder and trained the text side, and LLM2CLIP used an LLM to strengthen CLIP's text understanding for long, detailed captions, showing benefits once methods matured.

What is a good course on vision language models?

Good starting points include Hugging Face's community computer vision course, which covers multimodal models, DeepLearning.AI short courses on multimodal RAG and prompting vision models, and university lectures such as Stanford's CS231n on vision and language.

Also asked as: vision language action models course

Hands-on practice matters most: run an open VLM with Transformers or Ollama, compare models on your own images, and fine-tune a small one such as SmolVLM or PaliGemma on a narrow task with LoRA. Read the LLaVA and CLIP papers to understand the architecture. Hugging Face's blog posts on VLMs explain training stages and evaluation clearly. Build a project, such as extracting fields from receipts, and measure accuracy against labelled examples.

What are vision language action models, and where can you learn them?

Vision language action models, VLAs, extend VLMs to robotics: they take camera images and instructions and output robot actions. Examples include Google DeepMind's RT-2, the open OpenVLA, Physical Intelligence's pi-zero and Gemini Robotics.

To learn VLAs, start with Hugging Face's LeRobot library and tutorials, which provide datasets, pretrained policies and simulation environments, and read the RT-2 and OpenVLA papers. University robotics and deep reinforcement learning courses cover the control foundations. Practise in simulation first, then on affordable robot arms supported by LeRobot. VLAs face challenges VLMs do not, including safety, real-time control and the cost of collecting demonstration data, so expect slower, more hardware-dependent progress.
