How to invite a guest speaker to your college: the email, the one-pager, fees, logistics and what makes a speaker say yes

How do I invite a guest speaker to my college?

Send one clear email with six things: who you are and which club or department you represent, who will be in the room and how many, two or three proposed dates with a time window, the format and duration you want, the topic you hope they will cover and the outcome you want for students, and what you can offer, travel, a certificate, a recording, a meal. Then follow up once after five days. Speakers say yes to invitations that make the decision easy.

I receive these invitations regularly, and the ones I accept fastest share a shape. This is the template, written from the receiving end, with what actually decides a yes [1].

"Can you come and speak about AI?" is the most common invitation I get and the hardest to say yes to, because "AI" is not a talk. "Can you run a 90-minute session on building a RAG system for 60 third-years on 12 or 19 October?" is a talk. Pranjul Rathour

What should the invitation email say?

Subject: "Guest session on [topic] for [college], [month]". Body: one line on who you are, one on the audience, the two dates, the format, the topic and outcome, the offer, and a link to your club's page or last event. Under 200 words. Attach nothing; link instead. Sign with a name, a role and a phone number.

How do I find a guest speaker for a college event?

Start with alumni working in the field, then practitioners in your city, then people who already post about speaking at colleges on LinkedIn. Search LinkedIn for your city plus the topic and "workshop" or "session", check who has spoken at nearby colleges' events, ask community organisers at Google Developer Groups on Campus or Microsoft Learn Student Ambassadors chapters, and look at who judges local hackathons. People who already give their time to students are the ones who will give it to you.

Community programmes such as Google Developer Groups on Campus and Microsoft Learn Student Ambassadors exist partly to connect chapters with speakers, and their organisers know who is active in your region [6][7]. Major League Hacking's community does the same for hackathon-adjacent speakers [8].

Are guest speakers paid? What does a guest lecturer cost?

It varies. Many working engineers, including me, speak at student communities for free, on-site with travel covered or remote at no cost, because that is how we were helped. Professional speakers and senior executives often charge a fee. Universities sometimes have a fixed honorarium for guest lecturers under their academic rules. Ask directly in the first email: "We can offer travel and a certificate; if you have a fee, please tell us." Nobody is offended by that sentence.

My own rule: free for student communities and colleges, on-site anywhere in India with travel covered, remote anywhere at no cost [3]. The reason is not generosity; it is that the students in those rooms are exactly the people I want in the community and, later, in the industry.

What topics should I ask a speaker to cover?

Ask for a topic that ends with students able to do something, not knowing something. "Build a RAG system in 90 minutes", "How hackathon judges score, from a judge", "From BCA to GenAI engineer: the roadmap", "Ship your first AI app for free". Pick from the speaker's own menu if they publish one, and say which of your students it is for: first-years need a roadmap; final-years need a project and interview preparation.

What does a speaker need from the organisers?

A one-pager a week before the event: the exact venue and how to reach it, the audience size and year, start and end times, the schedule around their slot, the tech setup, projector input, Wi-Fi, microphone, whiteboard, two contact numbers, and what you will need from them, a bio, a photo, a title. On the day: someone to meet them, water, a working projector tested with their laptop, and a hard stop you protect.

The single most common failure I see is the projector: a laptop that will not connect, an adapter nobody has, ten minutes of the session gone. Test with the speaker's actual laptop, or ask them to send slides to run from yours.

How do I introduce a guest speaker?

In under a minute: their name, what they do now, one specific thing they have built or achieved that this audience will respect, why they are here today, and their name again. Do not read their whole LinkedIn. Do not list every award. One concrete credential lands harder than ten adjectives.

For example: "Pranjul Rathour is a GenAI engineer from Kanpur who shipped five production AI systems this year and won first prize at Changethon at IIT Roorkee. He did his BCA at this college. He's here to show you how a RAG system is actually built. Please welcome Pranjul." Forty seconds, done.

What is the difference between a guest lecture, a seminar, a workshop and a webinar?

A guest lecture is one expert speaking to a class, usually 45 to 90 minutes with questions. A seminar is a session built around discussion, often with several speakers or student presentations. A workshop is hands-on: students build or practise something with the speaker's guidance, and it needs laptops, more time and a smaller room. A webinar is any of these delivered online. Ask for the format that matches your goal: a lecture to inspire, a workshop to equip.

How do I get a speaker for a remote or online session?

The same email, minus travel, plus the platform you will use and whether you will record. Remote sessions are the easiest yes a speaker can give, so use them for speakers outside your city, for shorter formats and for follow-ups after an in-person session. Test the platform with the speaker for five minutes the day before, assign a moderator to read questions, and send the recording link within a week.

I speak remotely for any student community anywhere at no cost, which is true of many practitioners; the constraint is never the speaker's willingness, it is whether the invitation is concrete [3].

How do I turn one session into a lasting relationship?

Post the photos and a recap the same week, tagging the speaker and the college. Send a two-line thank-you with one thing students did as a result. Then ask for the next specific thing: judging your hackathon, a follow-up workshop, an office-hours call for the club, feedback on three student projects. Speakers remember organisers who follow through, and the second yes is always faster than the first.

Three of my longest mentoring relationships with student clubs started as one session; what they had in common was an organiser who wrote back a week later with photos and a question.

What mistakes do organisers make?

Vague topics. One date offered with no alternative. Asking two days before the event. No mention of the audience. Attachments instead of links. Forgetting to say whether the session is online or on-site. Not testing the projector. Ending late and eating into the speaker's next commitment. Never posting afterwards. Each is fixed by the checklist above, and each one I have experienced as a speaker.
