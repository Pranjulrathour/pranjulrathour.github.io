Mentoring students remotely and internationally: what a mentor actually does, how to find one, and how to run a mentorship program across time zones

By Pranjul RathourUpdated 2026-09-247 questions answered1,364 words

Key takeaways

  • A mentor's job is to ask the question that helps you see your own problem clearly, not to hand you the answer.
  • You do not need a formal program to have a mentor. A well-asked question to the right person, publicly or in a DM, starts most mentoring relationships.
  • {'Remote and international mentoring works best async-first': 'a recorded update beats a scheduling scramble across time zones.'}
  • Running a student mentorship program at scale needs structure, matching, a cadence, a way to end well, more than it needs enthusiasm alone.
  • Mentoring internationally taught me the same lessons travel more slowly through video calls than they do through a well-written async update.

What does a mentor actually do?

A mentor asks the question that helps you see your own problem clearly, rather than handing you a ready answer. A teacher covers a syllabus; a manager is responsible for your output; a mentor has no formal stake in the outcome and is there specifically to help you think, usually from having stood where you are standing a few years earlier. The best mentoring I have given or received was rarely advice; it was a question that made the actual problem obvious.

I have mentored more than two hundred students through TechVerse Enclave and campus sessions in Kanpur, and separately spoken to international student communities remotely, and the two experiences taught me the same core lesson at different speeds: good mentoring is mostly good questions, asked consistently, over time [4].

A mentor's best sentence is usually a question. The moment you are handing out answers faster than you are asking questions, you have become a lecturer, not a mentor. Pranjul Rathour

How do I find a mentor without a formal program?

Ask a specific, well-scoped question publicly or in a direct message to someone whose work you have actually engaged with, not a cold "can you mentor me". People say yes to a real question they can answer in ten minutes far more often than to an open-ended relationship they have to commit to first. A single good exchange, followed by another good question a few weeks later, is how most real mentoring relationships actually start, not a formal ask.

What does mentoring look like when it crosses time zones?

Async-first. A live call every week across a large time-zone gap is where most remote mentoring relationships die, because someone is always awake at 2 a.m. for it. What works instead: a recorded short video or a written update from the mentee on a fixed cadence, a written or recorded response from the mentor within an agreed window, and a live call reserved for when something genuinely needs back-and-forth. Speaking to international student communities taught me this directly: the sessions that stuck were the ones with a recording and a written follow-up, not the live call alone [8].

How do I run a student mentorship program, not just one relationship?

Structure beats enthusiasm at any real scale. Match mentors and mentees by a specific stated goal, not a vague pairing. Set a fixed cadence and a fixed duration, three months, not indefinite, with an explicit renewal decision at the end rather than a silent fade-out. Give mentors a light template for check-ins so quality does not depend entirely on how naturally each one writes. Build in an easy, non-awkward way to end a pairing that is not working, since forcing a bad match to continue helps nobody.

What is the difference between a mentor, a judge, and a speaker?

A mentor works with you over time on your own goals. A judge evaluates a specific piece of work against a rubric, once. A speaker delivers a session to a room and mostly does not follow up individually afterward. The same person often plays all three roles at different events, and knowing which hat is on matters: a judge who starts mentoring during judging is not being fair to other teams, and a speaker who tries to mentor an entire audience in one session will help nobody well.

What mistakes do mentors and mentees make?

Mentors: answering instead of asking, becoming a free labour source for the mentee's project instead of a thinking partner, and letting a mismatched pairing continue out of politeness. Mentees: asking questions a search engine would answer, expecting a mentor to have all the time a full course would take, and disappearing without closing the loop on advice they were given. Both sides: treating a single good conversation as a permanent relationship without maintaining it.

Mentoring interview and program-design questions

Explain what makes mentoring different from teaching or managing. Describe how you would structure a mentorship program so quality does not depend entirely on individual mentors' natural ability. Explain how you would run a mentoring relationship across a large time-zone gap. Describe how you would end a mismatched pairing without it feeling like a rejection. The strongest answer draws on a specific relationship, not a general theory of mentoring.

Where should I start?

If you want a mentor, send one specific, well-scoped question to someone whose work you have actually engaged with, this week. If you want to mentor others, commit to one person, one cadence, three months, and see it through before you try to scale it into a program.
