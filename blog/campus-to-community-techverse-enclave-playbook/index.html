From campus to community: the TechVerse Enclave playbook

How a college group became a 500+ member developer community: the first thirty members, the weekly rhythm, admins who own areas, sessions that convert lurkers, and what nearly killed it.

TechVerse Enclave grew out of a few students at VSICS helping each other ship and became a community of 500+ members across AI, web, cybersecurity, data and startups, with a LinkedIn community launched in September 2025 and 200+ students mentored along the way. None of that was luck; most of it was rhythm. This is the playbook, for anyone trying to do the same at their college.

Start with thirty, not three hundred

The first thirty members set the culture. We recruited people who had built something — anything — and asked them to post it. A community where the first thing you see is someone's project becomes a community where people post projects. One where the first thing you see is a motivational quote becomes a feed.

A weekly rhythm people can predict

  • One session a week, same day, same time — a build, a talk or a review.
  • One thread a week asking what people shipped, with admins answering every reply.
  • One external event a month: a hackathon to enter as teams, a speaker, a visit.

Admins who own areas

Six admins — Raman Shukla, Saksham Gupta, Nikhil Tiwari, Bidhan Bhattacharya, Om Tripathi and me — each owning a track: AI, web, cybersecurity, data, startups, operations. Ownership means the track has a session calendar and a person to ask. Shared ownership means nobody owns it.

Sessions that convert lurkers

Ninety percent of any community watches. The sessions that pulled people into participating were the ones with a hands-on block and a show-and-tell — the format in a tech club session format that works. Someone who has shown their work once on a projector posts it online the next week.

What nearly killed it

  • Growing membership faster than sessions. Numbers without rhythm produce silence.
  • Admin burnout around exams. The fix was a published calendar with owners, so a missed week was a known gap rather than a slow death.
  • Letting the channel become announcements only. Communities die quietly when members stop replying to each other.

What it made possible

Hackathon teams that already knew how to work together — Team TechVerse won three first prizes in 2025 — and a pipeline of students ready for the mentorship sessions we ran on campus. If you are starting a community at your college and want a session to kick it off, I do those; see how to start a tech community on your campus and the invite page.
