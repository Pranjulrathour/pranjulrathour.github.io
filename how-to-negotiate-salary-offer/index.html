How to negotiate a salary offer, even as a fresher: timing, percentage, scripts

How to negotiate a salary offer, whether freshers can, whether it is bad or common, what percentage to ask, going above range, timing, and raises after a year.

Key takeaways

  • Negotiate after you have a written offer, with market data and a specific, justified number.
  • Freshers can negotiate, though campus offers with fixed bands leave less room; ask about other parts of the package.
  • Polite negotiation is normal and rarely costs an offer; ultimatums and bluffing do.
  • Asking for roughly 10 to 20 percent above the offer is common when you can justify it.
  • For raises, document your impact over the year and ask in a dedicated conversation before budgets are set.

To negotiate a salary offer, wait until you have it in writing, research what the role pays, then thank the employer and ask for a specific, justified figure, framed around the value you bring and your enthusiasm for the job. Most employers expect some negotiation. I mentor students through placements and lead an engineering team at SCULT INDIA, so this page covers negotiating offers, freshers' options, whether negotiating is bad or common, what percentage to ask, going above the range, timing, and asking for a raise after a year.

How do I negotiate a salary offer?

Get the offer in writing, research market pay for the role, decide your target and walk-away numbers, then respond warmly with one specific request and a short reason. Negotiate the whole package, not just base pay.

Also asked as: how to negotiate salary offer

A simple script: "Thank you, I'm excited about this role. Based on market data for similar roles and my experience with X, I was hoping for a base closer to [figure]. Is there flexibility?" Then stop talking and listen. Consider joining bonus, variable pay, notice buyout, remote days, learning budget, title and start date. Knowing your alternative, such as another offer, gives you confidence. Get any agreed changes in a revised written offer.

Can freshers negotiate salary?

Yes, freshers can negotiate, but room varies. Campus placements with fixed packages for a batch usually leave little flexibility, while off-campus offers from startups and mid-size companies often have more.

Even when base pay is fixed, freshers can sometimes negotiate start date, location, joining bonus, relocation support, team or role, and training. Your leverage as a fresher comes from proof: internships, shipped projects, hackathon wins and competing offers. Students I mentor who had a live project and a clear demo had noticeably better conversations than those relying only on grades. If the answer is no, accept gracefully; how you negotiate is remembered more than whether you did.

How should a fresher negotiate salary?

Negotiate politely, with evidence, and only once you have an offer. Research the typical range for the role and city type, highlight specific skills or projects that justify more, and ask a question rather than making a demand.

Also asked as: how to negotiate salary as fresher

Steps for freshers:

  • Find ranges on Glassdoor, AmbitionBox and from seniors at the company.
  • List your proof: internships, projects with users, certifications, competing offers.
  • Ask: "Is there any flexibility on the base or a joining bonus?"
  • If base pay is fixed, ask about the review cycle and when a raise is possible.
  • Respond within the deadline and confirm everything in writing.

Never invent a competing offer; recruiters talk, and it can cost you the job.

How do freshers negotiate salary in an interview?

Avoid naming a number early in interviews if you can; ask about the budgeted range instead. If pressed, give a researched range whose bottom you would accept, and say you are flexible for the right role.

Also asked as: how to negotiate salary in interview for freshers

A good response to "What are your salary expectations?": "I'm focused on finding the right role and learning. Could you share the budgeted range for this position?" If they insist: "Based on my research for similar roles, a range of [figures] seems fair, but I'm open depending on the full package." Save real negotiation for after the offer, when the company has decided it wants you and you have the most leverage.

Is it bad to negotiate salary?

No. Polite, reasoned negotiation is professional and expected in most companies. It rarely leads to an offer being withdrawn; what damages relationships is aggressive ultimatums, dishonesty, or repeatedly reopening agreed terms.

Some situations call for care: government jobs and campus batches with fixed pay, very small organisations with tight budgets, or offers already at the top of a published band. In those cases, negotiate non-salary terms instead. Also negotiate once, comprehensively, rather than item by item. Employers often respect candidates who advocate for themselves calmly, because the same skill matters when working with clients and stakeholders.

Is it common to negotiate salary?

Yes, especially for experienced hires and in the private sector, where recruiters often build in some room expecting negotiation. Many candidates still accept the first offer, often from anxiety rather than because the offer was final.

Norms vary by company type: large firms have structured bands and approval processes, startups may trade salary for equity or flexibility, and campus programmes are mostly standardised. Women and first-generation professionals are often less likely to negotiate, which compounds pay gaps over time, since future raises build on the starting figure. Asking once, respectfully, is normal.

What percentage should I negotiate on a salary offer?

A common range is asking for roughly 10 to 20 percent above the initial offer, if market data and your experience support it. Smaller asks suit offers already near market rate; larger ones need strong justification, such as a competing offer.

Also asked as: what percentage to negotiate salary

Base your number on research, not a fixed rule: if the offer is well below market, asking for more than 20 percent can be reasonable; if it is already generous, a smaller increase or better benefits may be smarter. Ask for a specific figure rather than a range, because people usually settle near the bottom of any range you give. Expect the company to meet you partway.

Can I negotiate above the salary range?

Sometimes. Ranges are often set by budgets and bands, but exceptional candidates, scarce skills, competing offers or a more senior title can justify going above. Companies can also move you to a higher band or add a joining bonus.

Also asked as: can you negotiate above salary range

If an offer is at the top of the range, ask whether the role could be levelled up given your experience, or whether a one-time bonus, extra leave or an early salary review is possible. Be realistic: some organisations, especially with published pay scales, cannot exceed their bands. If the gap between the range and your needs is large, it may not be the right role.

When should I negotiate salary for a new job?

Negotiate after you receive a written offer and before you accept it. That is when your leverage is highest: the company has chosen you, and you have not committed. Respond within the stated deadline.

Also asked as: when to negotiate salary for new job

Before the offer, discuss expectations in general terms; after accepting, reopening pay damages trust. If you need time, ask: "Could I have until Friday to review the full offer?" If you are waiting for another company's result, tell them you have an offer with a deadline; they may speed up. Have the conversation on a call where possible, then confirm in writing.

How do I negotiate a salary raise after a year?

Document your impact over the year with specific results, research current market pay for your role, and ask your manager for a dedicated conversation, ideally before the annual budget or review cycle is finalised.

Also asked as: how to negotiate salary after a year

Prepare a one-page summary: projects delivered, problems solved, responsibilities added, and measurable outcomes such as time saved or revenue influenced. Then say: "I'd like to discuss my compensation given my contributions this year." If the answer is "not now", ask what specific results would justify a raise and when you can revisit it, and write that down. Sometimes a title change or new responsibilities come first, with pay following at the next cycle.
