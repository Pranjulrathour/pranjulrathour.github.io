How to do side projects: meaning, design, ideas, finishing, marketing and grades

A side project is something you build outside your main studies or job, for learning, fun, a portfolio or a possible business. For developers and students, side projects are often the clearest proof of skill. My own products, from KrishGyan and Annapurna to the five NextUpgrad AI apps, were built alongside my BCA studies, so this page covers what side projects mean, how to start and design one, ideas, grades versus projects, community views, why projects stall, when to commit, and marketing them.

What does side project mean?

A side project is work you do in addition to your primary job or studies, usually self-directed: an app, tool, website, open-source library, blog or experiment. It is driven by interest or learning rather than obligation.

In music, a side project is a band or album that members create apart from their main group, which explains some unrelated search results. In tech, side projects range from weekend experiments to products that become companies; several well-known products started as side projects inside or outside companies. For students, side projects show initiative and practical skill beyond coursework, and they give you real stories for interviews about decisions, bugs and trade-offs you handled yourself.

How do you do a side project?

Pick a small problem you care about, define the smallest version that is useful, build it in short regular sessions, ship it publicly, and improve it based on feedback. Finishing a small project beats abandoning an ambitious one.

Also asked as: how to side project · why you never shipped that side project

Practical habits: schedule fixed time, such as two evenings a week; keep a short task list; use familiar tools so you spend time building, not configuring; deploy early with free tiers; and write a README as you go. Share progress in public to stay accountable. Set a deadline for version one, about two to four weeks, and cut features ruthlessly to hit it. Afterwards, decide whether to continue, polish or move on.

How do you design a side project?

Start with the user and one core job: who is it for, what problem does it solve, and what single flow proves value. Sketch that flow on paper, list the minimum features, and choose a simple architecture you can build and run cheaply.

Also asked as: how to design side project

A design checklist: a one-sentence purpose; user stories for the core flow; rough wireframes; a data model; API and storage choices; a deployment plan; and what you will deliberately leave out. Prefer boring, proven technology, such as FastAPI, Next.js and PostgreSQL, unless learning a new tool is the goal. Design for change: keep components simple and decoupled, add tests around critical logic, and log errors so you can debug once real users arrive.

What are good side project ideas?

Good ideas solve a problem you or people near you actually have: a study planner for your course, a RAG assistant over college notes, an expense splitter for hostel friends, a local language voice bot, or a tool that automates a boring task.

Developer-focused ideas include a CLI tool, a browser extension, a GitHub Action, an API wrapper or an open-source library improvement. AI ideas include a document question answering app with citations, a fine-tuned small model for a narrow task, or an agent that triages your email. Pick ideas where you can get real users, even ten, since feedback teaches more than features. Avoid cloning popular apps unless you add a clear twist.

What are the best side projects for senior software developers?

Senior developers benefit most from projects that stretch them in new directions: learning a new domain such as AI, contributing to important open-source projects, building tools that solve team problems, writing or teaching, and small products with real users.

Also asked as: best side projects reddit

Options include building infrastructure tools others adopt, maintaining an open-source library, publishing deep technical writing, mentoring through community projects, or launching a niche software product to learn sales and marketing. Exploring AI engineering through a real application, such as a RAG system with evaluation, keeps skills current. Seniors should choose projects that compound reputation and learning, and keep scope realistic given limited time, family and work commitments.

Low marks or side projects: which matters more?

Community discussions usually say strong side projects can outweigh average marks for many software jobs, especially at startups, but very low marks can block campus placements and some employers that filter by grades. Aim for both where possible.

Also asked as: ask hn: low marks vs. side projects

Practical balance: keep marks above common cutoffs used by recruiters in your context, often around 60 to 70 percent in Indian campus drives, while investing most extra time in projects that demonstrate skill. Explain trade-offs honestly in interviews, showing what you built and learned. Postgraduate admissions and some government roles weigh marks more heavily, so consider your goals. Projects open doors marks cannot, but marks can close doors before anyone sees your projects.

Why do side projects remain unfinished?

Common reasons are scope creep, perfectionism, choosing new technology that slows progress, losing motivation after the exciting start, and burnout from adding projects on top of full workloads. Most unfinished projects never had a clear, small definition of done.

Fixes: define version one as a single core flow and ship it within weeks; keep a done list to see progress; use tools you know; work in short, regular sessions instead of occasional marathons; and share progress publicly for accountability. If motivation fades, ask whether the project still serves your goals, and archive it guilt-free if not; abandoning deliberately is better than dragging it on. Rest matters too, since tired people do not ship.

When is a side project worth committing to?

Commit when there are signals beyond your own excitement: real users returning, people asking for features, measurable learning you value, or a clear path to income or career benefit, and when you can sustain the time without hurting your main responsibilities.

Test before committing: ship a minimal version, share it with a small audience, and watch behaviour rather than compliments. Weekly active users, retention and unsolicited feedback are strong signals. Consider opportunity cost: what else could the same hours achieve? If signals are strong, set goals for the next three months and review them. If the project is mainly for learning, commitment can be lighter: finish, document and move on when the lessons are captured.

What is on a side project marketing checklist?

A simple checklist: a clear one-line description, a landing page with screenshots or a demo video, a README for developer tools, a launch post in relevant communities, and a way to collect emails or feedback.

More items: post on platforms where your users gather, such as Reddit communities, Product Hunt, Hacker News or LinkedIn; write a short article explaining why you built it and what you learned; share progress updates; add basic analytics; set up SEO basics such as titles, descriptions and a sitemap; and respond quickly to early users. Marketing is ongoing, not one launch day, so plan a few posts over the following weeks.

What does the community say about side projects?

Community discussions highlight side projects that solved the creator's own problem, taught a valuable skill, or found a small loyal audience. Popular advice is to build things you would use yourself and to finish small projects before starting new ones.

Frequently praised examples include personal finance trackers, browser extensions, automation scripts, niche directories, open-source developer tools and small AI utilities. Many developers say their side projects led to jobs, freelance clients or startups, while others valued the learning most. Common warnings include spending months on features nobody asked for and neglecting health. The shared lesson: start small, ship publicly, listen to users, and treat every project as a learning investment.
