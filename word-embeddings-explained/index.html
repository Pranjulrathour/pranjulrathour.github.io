Word embeddings explained: meaning, how they are made, dimensions and cosine

Key takeaways

  • 'Embeddings' is a real English word, the plural of embedding; in machine learning it means vector representations.
  • Word embeddings map words to dense vectors so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors.
  • They are learned from large text corpora with methods such as word2vec, GloVe and fastText, or inside transformers.
  • Typical sizes range from 50 to 300 dimensions for classic models and 384 to 4,096 for modern ones.
  • For most new projects, use contextual sentence embeddings rather than static word vectors.

Yes, "embeddings" is a word: the plural of "embedding", meaning something fixed firmly into a surrounding mass. In machine learning, an embedding is a vector of numbers representing an item, and word embeddings represent words so that similar meanings sit close together. They underpin search, recommendations and the retrieval in RAG systems such as RAG.NextUpgrad. This page explains what word embeddings mean, how they are created and help, their dimensions, word order, cosine similarity, fine-tuning, which to choose, and how to learn them.

Is "embeddings" a word?

Yes. "Embedding" is a standard English noun derived from the verb "embed", and "embeddings" is its plural. Dictionaries define embedding as the act of fixing something within a surrounding mass, or the thing embedded.

Also asked as: is embeddings a word

In mathematics, an embedding is a way of placing one structure inside another while preserving its properties. Machine learning borrowed the term for mapping words, sentences, images or users into a vector space where geometry reflects relationships. So "word embeddings", "sentence embeddings" and "image embeddings" are all correct technical terms, and spell checkers that flag "embeddings" are simply missing technical vocabulary.

What does "word embeddings" mean?

Word embeddings are dense vectors of real numbers that represent words, learned so that words appearing in similar contexts have similar vectors. "Doctor" and "physician" end up close together; "doctor" and "bicycle" far apart.

Also asked as: what does word embeddings mean

Unlike one-hot encoding, where each word is an isolated position in a huge sparse vector, embeddings capture relationships compactly. Famous examples show directions with meaning: the vector from "man" to "woman" resembles the one from "king" to "queen". Classic word embeddings give each word one fixed vector. Modern contextual embeddings from transformers give a word different vectors depending on its sentence, so "bank" in "river bank" and "bank loan" differ.

How are word embeddings created?

Word embeddings are learned from large amounts of text by training a model to predict words from their contexts, or contexts from words, so the vectors that make predictions work become the embeddings. Word2vec, GloVe and fastText are the classic methods.

Word2vec, from Google in 2013, trains a shallow network with either CBOW, predicting a word from neighbours, or skip-gram, predicting neighbours from a word. GloVe, from Stanford, factorises global word co-occurrence statistics. FastText adds character n-grams so it can build vectors for rare and misspelled words. In transformers, an embedding layer is learned along with the whole model, and contextual vectors come from later layers.

How do word embeddings help?

Word embeddings help models understand that different words can mean similar things, which improves search, classification, recommendations and translation. They give neural networks meaningful, compact inputs instead of arbitrary word IDs.

Practical benefits: semantic search finds "cheap flights" for "low-cost airfare"; classifiers generalise from fewer labelled examples because similar words share information; spelling variants and synonyms are handled better; and vectors can be compared, clustered and visualised. In RAG systems, embeddings of chunks and questions drive retrieval, though combining them with keyword search catches exact names and codes that embeddings can blur.

How many dimensions do word embeddings have?

Classic word embeddings typically use 50 to 300 dimensions; 300 was common for word2vec and GloVe. Modern sentence and document embeddings range from 384 to around 4,096 dimensions, depending on the model.

Also asked as: how many dimensions are word embeddings

More dimensions can capture finer distinctions but cost more memory and compute, and returns diminish. For search over millions of chunks, a 384 or 768-dimension model is often a good balance; some models support truncating vectors to fewer dimensions with little quality loss. Each dimension is not individually interpretable as a single meaning; information is spread across them. Whatever you choose, the index must use the same dimension as the model's output.

Can word embeddings ignore the order of words?

Static word embeddings themselves carry no order information; each word has one vector regardless of position. Simple methods that average word vectors into a sentence vector do ignore order, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look identical.

Models that need order add it: recurrent networks read words in sequence, convolutional networks see local windows, and transformers add positional encodings to word embeddings before attention. Modern sentence embedding models are transformer-based, so they distinguish most order-dependent meanings, though subtle differences can still produce very similar vectors. Test with examples that matter for your application, such as negations.

Why is cosine distance used to measure similarity between word embeddings?

Cosine similarity compares the direction of two vectors, ignoring their length. For embeddings, direction carries meaning while length often reflects word frequency or other factors, so cosine gives a more reliable measure of semantic similarity than raw distance.

Also asked as: why is the cosine distance used to measure the similatiry between word embeddings

Cosine similarity is the dot product of two vectors divided by the product of their lengths, ranging from minus one to one. Cosine distance is one minus that value. Many embedding models are trained with cosine-based objectives, so cosine matches how they were optimised. If vectors are normalised to unit length, cosine similarity equals the dot product, and ranking by Euclidean distance gives the same order, which is why vector databases often normalise vectors.

Are word embeddings updated during training for document classification?

They can be either. You can freeze pretrained embeddings, keeping them fixed while training the classifier, or fine-tune them along with the model. Fine-tuning often helps with enough data; freezing is safer with small datasets.

Also asked as: are word embeddings further updated during training for document classification

With few labelled examples, updating embeddings can overfit and distort words that appear rarely in training. With larger datasets, fine-tuning adapts vectors to domain meanings, such as "positive" in medical reports. A common strategy is to freeze at first, then unfreeze later with a lower learning rate. With transformers, the standard approach is fine-tuning the whole model, or using adapters such as LoRA, rather than separate word vectors.

What is the point of word embeddings, and which should I use?

The point is to represent meaning numerically so software can compare, search and learn from text. For most new projects, use a modern sentence embedding model rather than classic word vectors, because it captures context and works directly on sentences and passages.

Also asked as: what's the point of word embeddings? and which one should i use for my project

Guidance:

  • Semantic search or RAG: a sentence-transformers model or a hosted embedding API, chosen by testing on your queries.
  • Multilingual content: a multilingual embedding model.
  • Low resources or offline: small sentence models, or fastText for word-level work.
  • Teaching and analysis: word2vec or GloVe, which are easy to explore.

Evaluate two or three models on your own data before committing.

Is there a good word embeddings tutorial?

Yes. TensorFlow's word embeddings guide trains embeddings in a classification model, the Gensim library has word2vec tutorials, Stanford's CS224N lectures cover the theory, and the Sentence Transformers documentation teaches modern sentence embeddings for search.

A learning path:

  • Train word2vec with Gensim on a small corpus and explore nearest neighbours.
  • Visualise vectors with PCA or the TensorFlow Embedding Projector.
  • Build a classifier with an embedding layer.
  • Switch to Sentence Transformers and build semantic search over your own notes.
  • Compare cosine results with keyword search on the same questions.

Building something small end to end teaches more than reading theory alone.
