Computer vision basics: human vs machine, coarse vs fine, distance, roadmap, courses

Key takeaways

  • Computer vision learns visual patterns from data; human vision adds context and common sense.
  • Machine vision is industrial inspection and guidance; computer vision is the broader field.
  • Coarse-to-fine methods solve problems roughly at low resolution, then refine at high resolution.
  • Camera distance comes from the pinhole model with a known size, or from stereo, depth sensors or depth networks.
  • Learn OpenCV, PyTorch, detection and deployment through courses like CS231n and real projects.

Computer vision is the field of AI that lets computers understand images and video: classifying, detecting, segmenting, tracking, reading text and estimating depth. I built FaceVision, which runs face detection, recognition and liveness in the browser with ONNX Runtime Web and stores only embeddings, plus OCR pipelines for documents, so this page compares human, machine and computer vision and NLP, explains coarse and fine, camera distance, and gives a roadmap, tutorials, courses, books and examples.

Human vs computer vision: how do they differ?

Human vision combines eyes with a brain that interprets scenes effortlessly using context, memory and common sense. Computer vision uses cameras and algorithms, usually deep neural networks, that learn patterns from data but lack general understanding.

Humans recognise objects from few examples, handle unusual lighting and viewpoints, and understand intent and physics. Computers excel at speed, consistency and scale: inspecting thousands of parts per minute, reading text accurately, or measuring precisely. They fail in ways humans would not, such as being fooled by small adversarial changes or by objects outside their training data. In practice, the best systems pair computer vision for scale with human review for ambiguous or high-stakes cases.

Machine vision vs computer vision: what is the difference?

Machine vision usually refers to industrial systems that inspect, measure or guide machines on production lines, with controlled lighting, dedicated cameras and fast, reliable decisions. Computer vision is the broader scientific field of making computers understand images.

Also asked as: machine vs computer vision

Machine vision applications include defect detection, barcode and label reading, robot guidance and dimension measurement, often running on specialised hardware with strict timing. Computer vision covers those plus open-world problems: self-driving perception, medical imaging, face recognition, augmented reality and image generation. The boundary is blurring as deep learning enters factories. If you work in manufacturing, machine vision skills include optics and lighting design, not just models, because a well-lit image solves half the problem.

NLP vs computer vision: which should you learn?

NLP processes text and speech; computer vision processes images and video. Both use deep learning, increasingly transformers, and they converge in multimodal models that read images and text together. Choose based on the problems that excite you.

NLP currently has more applied demand through LLMs, RAG and agents, while computer vision is strong in manufacturing, healthcare, retail, security and autonomous systems, often with edge deployment. Skills overlap: data preparation, model training, evaluation and deployment transfer between them. Many real products combine both, such as document AI, which uses OCR and layout vision with language models. My own work spans both, from FaceVision to RAG.NextUpgrad and DocuLens AI.

What do "coarse" and "fine" mean in computer vision?

Coarse means low resolution or broad detail; fine means high resolution or precise detail. Coarse-to-fine methods first solve a problem roughly on downscaled images or large regions, then refine the answer at higher resolution.

Also asked as: what do the words "coarse" and "fine" mean in the context of computer vision

Examples: image pyramids for optical flow and registration estimate motion on small images first, then refine at full resolution; detection models predict coarse boxes, then refine them; segmentation networks combine coarse semantic features from deep layers with fine spatial detail from shallow layers, as in U-Net skip connections. In classification, coarse-grained means broad categories like vehicle, while fine-grained means subtle categories like specific car models or bird species, which are much harder to tell apart.

How do you calculate the distance between a camera and an object?

With a calibrated camera and an object of known size, use the pinhole model: distance equals real width times focal length in pixels, divided by width in pixels. A 20 cm object 100 pixels wide at a 1,000-pixel focal length is 200 cm away.

Also asked as: how to calculate the distance between the camera and an object using computer vision

Find the focal length by calibrating the camera with a checkerboard in OpenCV, which also corrects lens distortion. Without known object sizes, use a stereo camera pair, where depth comes from disparity between left and right images, a depth sensor such as LiDAR or time-of-flight, or a monocular depth estimation network, which predicts relative depth and needs scaling for metric distance. Accuracy falls with distance and poor detection boxes, so validate with measured ground truth.

What is a computer vision roadmap?

Learn Python, NumPy and basic linear algebra; then image processing with OpenCV; then deep learning with PyTorch and CNNs; then detection, segmentation and tracking; then transformers and multimodal models; and finally deployment on servers, edge devices and browsers.

Also asked as: roadmap computer vision

Milestones: build an image classifier with transfer learning; train a YOLO detector on a custom dataset; segment objects with a pretrained model; add tracking to count objects in video; export a model to ONNX and run it in a browser or on a Raspberry Pi; and build a vision-language feature, such as visual question answering. roadmap.sh and course syllabi provide structured checklists, but projects with real data teach the practical skills employers value most.

What is a good computer vision tutorial?

Start with OpenCV's official Python tutorials for image basics, then PyTorch's transfer learning tutorial for classification, then Ultralytics' YOLO guides for detection. Each has runnable code and moves from simple to practical quickly.

Also asked as: tutorial computer vision

A first-week plan: load and display images, convert colour spaces, blur, threshold, and find edges and contours with OpenCV; then capture webcam video and draw on frames. Next week, fine-tune a pretrained ResNet on a small dataset of your own photos. Then label 100 images in a tool such as Label Studio or Roboflow and train a detector. Free PDFs of classic texts and course notes, such as Stanford's CS231n notes, support the theory.

What are the best computer vision courses?

Strong options include Stanford's CS231n lectures and notes on deep learning for vision, the University of Michigan's deep learning for computer vision course, fast.ai's practical deep learning, OpenCV's courses, and Hugging Face's community computer vision course.

Choose by goal: CS231n and the Michigan course for depth and theory; fast.ai for building working models quickly; OpenCV courses for classical techniques and deployment; and Hugging Face's course for transformers and modern models. Supplement with Ultralytics documentation for detection projects. Whatever course you pick, complete its assignments and then build an independent project, since recruiters value a deployed vision application more than a certificate.

What are the best computer vision books?

Classic choices include Richard Szeliski's Computer Vision: Algorithms and Applications, which is free online, Hartley and Zisserman's Multiple View Geometry for 3D vision, and Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville's Deep Learning for neural network foundations.

For practical learning, books on deep learning with PyTorch and OpenCV-focused cookbooks help you build quickly. Forsyth and Ponce's Computer Vision: A Modern Approach is another respected textbook. Pair theory with code: after reading about camera models, calibrate your own camera; after reading about CNNs, train one. Because the field moves fast, supplement books with recent papers and documentation for modern detectors, segmentation models and vision transformers.

What are some examples of computer vision?

Everyday examples include face unlock on phones, document scanning and OCR, photo search by content, barcode and QR code reading, self-checkout systems, medical image analysis, quality inspection in factories, traffic cameras, and driver assistance in cars.

Also asked as: examples computer vision

Other examples: crop disease detection from leaf photos, counting people in stores, sports analytics tracking players, augmented reality filters, satellite imagery analysis for agriculture and disasters, and wildlife monitoring with camera traps. FaceVision, which I built, shows a privacy-first example: face detection, recognition and liveness checks run in the browser with ONNX Runtime Web, and only embeddings are stored in PostgreSQL, never images. Choose project ideas where vision solves a real problem.
