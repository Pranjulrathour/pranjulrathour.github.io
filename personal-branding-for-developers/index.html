Personal branding for developers and CS students: building in public, LinkedIn and GitHub that get you hired, and speaking your way into a reputation

By Pranjul RathourUpdated 2026-09-2410 questions answered1,766 words

Key takeaways

  • A personal brand for a developer is a public record of shipped work, explained clearly, on a schedule. It is not a logo or a tagline.
  • Post what you built this week, what broke and what you learned. Honest build logs outperform polished announcements for students.
  • LinkedIn is where Indian recruiters and organisers look; GitHub is where they verify; a personal site is where it all resolves.
  • One project becomes a post, a talk, a carousel and a session. Reuse is how a student keeps up with a weekly rhythm.
  • Consistency beats virality. Twelve months of weekly posts built more opportunities for me than any single post ever did.

What is personal branding for a developer, really?

A public, searchable record of what you have built, explained clearly enough that a stranger can judge it, published on a schedule. That is the whole thing. It is not a logo, a colour palette or a tagline. Recruiters, founders and event organisers search for people who can do a specific thing; a brand is what makes you findable for that thing and credible when found. Mine is "GenAI engineer from Kanpur who ships production AI systems and speaks at colleges", and every post, repository and talk feeds that one sentence.

What should a developer post about?

What you built this week, what broke, what you learned, and what you would do differently. A build log with a screenshot beats an announcement. A failure explained beats a success declared. Specific beats general: "I cut RAG latency from 4 seconds to 1.2 by reranking to five passages" is a post; "AI is transforming everything" is noise. Post about the work, the decision and the result, in that order, and tag the tools and people involved when it is true.

Which platforms matter for a developer in India?

LinkedIn, because recruiters, founders, faculty and event organisers in India live there and search there; it is where every speaking and hiring conversation I have had began [1][10]. GitHub, because it is where anyone serious verifies the claims [2]. A personal site, because it is the one place you control where everything resolves [3]. Then one writing home, Dev.to or a blog, for longer pieces [11]. X, Instagram and others are optional amplifiers. Two platforms done consistently beat six done occasionally.

How should a CS student set up LinkedIn?

Headline: what you do and one proof, not your college and year. Banner: a photo from a session, a demo or a hackathon, not a stock image. About: five lines, first person, what you build, one result, what you are open to, how to reach you. Featured: three projects with demo links. Experience: projects and communities count; write them as outcomes. Post weekly. Comment substantively on ten posts a week in your field, because comments are how people without followers get seen.

How should a developer set up GitHub?

A profile README with your one sentence, your city, and three links. Five pinned repositories, each with a README that has the problem in one sentence, a screenshot or demo link, how to run it, and what you would do next. Steady commits over weeks rather than one upload per project. Archive tutorial clones. Recruiters spend under two minutes; those minutes should land on finished things with clear explanations.

How do I write technical posts without a following?

Start with what you just learned, because a beginner explaining a concept to last-month's self is the most useful writer for the many people at that stage. Structure: the problem, what you tried, what worked, what you would tell someone starting. Include one screenshot or code block. Publish on Dev.to or your blog and post a three-line summary with the link on LinkedIn. Nobody reads the first ten; the eleventh gets found by search, and search does not care about followers.

How do I turn projects into talks and a speaker brand?

Every shipped project is a talk waiting for a room. Offer your college club a session on what you built, with a live demo, then post the photos. Two or three sessions become a page listing the talks you offer, and that page is what organisers book from. Judging a hackathon after mentoring at one adds a second credential. Within a year, invitations arrive with the posts, because organisers search LinkedIn for people who have visibly done sessions before.

How do I stay consistent without it eating my week?

Reuse and schedule. One project produces a post, a demo video, a carousel, a talk and a blog piece; write once, cut five ways. Batch: one afternoon a month to draft four posts, scheduled weekly. Keep a running notes file of things that broke and lessons learned, so posts are extracts, not blank pages. Automate what can be automated, publishing and cross-posting, and keep the writing human. My own posting runs on a pipeline I built for exactly this, so the habit survives busy months.

What mistakes do students make with personal branding?

Posting certificates instead of projects. Announcing instead of showing. Chasing virality with hot takes. Six platforms, none consistent. A LinkedIn headline that says only the college. Copying influencer formats that do not fit a student. Posting nothing for months and then a flood. Faking expertise, which experts spot instantly and never forget. The fix for all of them is the same weekly build log, in your own words.

Personal branding interview questions

Not asked directly, but present in every interview: "tell me about a project" is easier when you have written about it ten times; "how do you learn" is answered by your posts; "why should we hire you" is answered by a profile the interviewer already read. The brand is the pre-read. Make it accurate.
