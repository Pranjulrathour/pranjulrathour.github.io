How to start a tech community on campus: from five friends to 500 members, the sessions, the structure and the mistakes, from building TechVerse Enclave

By Pranjul RathourUpdated 2026-09-2410 questions answered1,566 words

Key takeaways

  • A community is a session that happens every week, not a group chat. Consistency beats promotion.
  • Start with five people and one topic you can teach. Grow by the members teaching next.
  • Roles, a calendar and a handover plan are what keep a community alive after its founders graduate.
  • Speakers, judges and sponsors say yes to communities with a track record of sessions; build the record first.
  • {'The community is the career accelerator nobody puts on a syllabus': 'teammates, clients, referrals and invitations all came from it.'}

How do I start a tech community on campus?

Pick one topic you can teach for an hour, book a room, tell five people, and run the session this week. Then run one next week. A community is a weekly session that happens whether five or fifty people attend; everything else, the name, the logo, the group chat, the sponsors, comes after the habit exists. TechVerse Enclave started in 2023 as a hackathon team and a handful of friends learning web development together. It has since grown past 500 members, and I have mentored more than 200 students through it. The first six months were the hardest and the most important.

Nobody joins a community. They come to a session, and they come back to the next one because it happened. The community is the coming back. Pranjul Rathour, founder of TechVerse Enclave

What should the first session be?

Something you built last month, shown live, then a hands-on hour where everyone leaves with a version running. A first session about "introduction to AI" attracts fifty curious people who never return. A first session where ten people deploy their first web page attracts ten people who return with friends. Teach what you know; the second session can be taught by whoever learned fastest.

The session format I still use, with timings, is written up separately [4]: ten minutes of story, twenty of demonstration, forty of hands-on, and a public artefact from every attendee.

How do I get people to show up and keep coming?

Same time, same place, every week, announced the day before, with photos and a recap the same day. Make the first-timer's experience good: someone greets them, the setup instructions were sent in advance, they leave with something working. Ask every attendee to bring one person. Celebrate small ships publicly. Growth came for TechVerse Enclave when members started teaching, because each new teacher brought their own circle.

How should a student community be organised?

Small roles with names: a lead, a sessions lead who owns the calendar, a content lead who posts, an outreach lead who invites speakers and talks to faculty, and a treasurer if money exists. A shared calendar planned a month ahead. A written handover document from day one, because the founders graduate. A faculty coordinator who signs forms and books rooms, kept informed monthly. Nothing more until the weekly session has run for a semester.

The mistake that kills most college clubs is not low attendance; it is a founding batch that graduates without a handover. The lessons post covers what I got wrong here first [2].

How do I get speakers, mentors and judges?

Invite practitioners with a concrete ask: a topic from their own work, two dates, the audience, the format, what you offer. Start with alumni and engineers in your city, then people who already post about speaking at colleges. Many, including me, speak for free for student communities. Build a record first: a speaker says yes faster to a community with photos of ten past sessions than to one with a plan. Then ask each speaker for the next thing, judging your hackathon, monthly office hours.

How do I run a hackathon as a student community?

Start with a 12-hour internal build day for your own members, then a one-day campus hackathon, then a bigger one with outside teams. Fix the rubric and the timeline before you fix the theme. Recruit judges who have built things and brief them for fifteen minutes. Protect the demo slot: three minutes each, a hard stop, a working projector. Sponsors come after the first successful event, not before. TechVerse Enclave's first hackathon was an afternoon in a classroom; the ones after had partners because the first one had photos.

How do I get sponsors, funding and partner programmes?

Programmes first, money second. Google Developer Groups on Campus, Microsoft Learn Student Ambassadors, the GitHub Campus Program and Major League Hacking give student communities structure, resources, swag, speakers and credibility, for free [6][7][8][9]. Apply once you have a semester of sessions to show. Local companies sponsor food and prizes for hackathons when you show them last year's photos and this year's plan. College funding follows visible activity, so make the activity visible first.

How does a community help your career?

In every way that a certificate does not. My teammates for three winning hackathons came from it. My first clients came from it. The sessions I ran inside it became the sessions colleges invited me to give. The people I mentored became referrals. Recruiters read "founded a 500-member developer community and mentored 200 students" as leadership evidence no course provides. And teaching every week is the fastest way to actually understand what you are learning.

What mistakes do new communities make?

Spending the first month on a name and a logo. Announcing a big event before running a small session. Founders doing everything and burning out. No handover, so the club dies with a batch. Chasing sponsors before there is anything to sponsor. Measuring the group chat's size instead of the room's. Every one of these I made or watched; the fix for all of them is the weekly session.
